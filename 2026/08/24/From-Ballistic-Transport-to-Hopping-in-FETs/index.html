From Ballistic Transport to Hopping in FETs

Quantum Transport Device Physics Field-Effect Transistors

Ballistic conduction, Schottky injection, tunneling, and hopping are often treated as separate transport models. This article connects them through one question: which electronic states are available, and how likely is an injected carrier to reach the drain? Starting from one-dimensional quantum states, it derives carrier flux and the Landauer formula, then follows transmission through Schottky barriers into thermionic emission, field emission, and thermionic-field emission. It concludes with localized-state transport, nearest-neighbor and variable-range hopping, mobility edges, and practical signatures for identifying the dominant mechanism in real FETs.

Semiconductor transport is often taught as a collection of separate models: ballistic transport, Schottky emission, tunneling, thermionic-field emission, nearest-neighbor hopping, and variable-range hopping.

A more useful picture is to connect them through one question:

What electronic states are available, and what is the probability that an electron injected from the source can reach the drain?

This leads to the hierarchy

[
\boxed{\text{Quantum states}\rightarrow\text{1D carrier flux}\rightarrow\text{Landauer transport}\rightarrow\mathcal T(E)\rightarrow\text{Schottky barrier}\rightarrow\text{TE/TFE/FE}\rightarrow\text{localized states}\rightarrow\text{hopping}}
]

Here, (\mathcal T(E)) is the energy-dependent transmission probability.

1. Quantum states and one-dimensional carrier flux

Consider an ideal one-dimensional conductor of length (L). For a plane wave

[
\psi(x)=e^{ikx},
]

periodic boundary conditions require

[
\psi(x+L)=\psi(x),
]

so

[
e^{ikL}=1,
\qquad
kL=2\pi n.
]

Therefore

[
\boxed{k_n=\frac{2\pi n}{L}}
]

and

[
\boxed{\Delta k=\frac{2\pi}{L}}.
]

For one spin species, the number of states between (k) and (k+dk) is

[
dN=\frac{L}{2\pi}dk.
]

This counts states. Transport instead asks how many states cross a section per unit time.

1.1 Why a 1D mode carries (dE/h) states per second

The group velocity is

[
v(k)=\frac{1}{\hbar}\frac{dE}{dk}.
]

An electron moving with speed (v) through a system of length (L) crosses a given point at a rate (v/L). Hence

[
d\dot N=dN\frac{v}{L}.
]

Using (dN=Ldk/(2\pi)),

[
d\dot N=\frac{v}{2\pi}dk.
]

Since

[
v,dk=\frac{dE}{\hbar},
]

we obtain

[
d\dot N=\frac{dE}{2\pi\hbar}=\boxed{\frac{dE}{h}}.
]

Thus a single propagating 1D quantum mode, for one spin and one propagation direction, carries (dE/h) available states per unit time in an energy interval (dE).

The result is independent of the detailed band dispersion because in one dimension

[
D_{1D}(E)\propto \frac{1}{v(E)},
]

so (D_{1D}(E)v(E)) is constant.

2. Landauer current and transport dimensionality

Connect the channel between source and drain reservoirs with Fermi functions (f_S(E)) and (f_D(E)). If an electron at energy (E) has transmission probability (\mathcal T(E)), then for one non-degenerate mode

[
dI=q\frac{dE}{h}\mathcal T(E)[f_S(E)-f_D(E)].
]

Therefore

[
\boxed{I=\frac{q}{h}\int \mathcal T(E)[f_S-f_D]dE}.
]

Including spin degeneracy gives

[
\boxed{I=\frac{2q}{h}\int \mathcal T(E)[f_S-f_D]dE}.
]

For (M(E)) transverse modes,

[
\boxed{I=\frac{2q}{h}\int M(E)\mathcal T(E)[f_S-f_D]dE}.
]

At low temperature and small bias,

[
\mu_S-\mu_D=qV,
]

so

[
I\approx \frac{2q^2}{h}M(E_F)\mathcal T(E_F)V.
]

Hence

[
\boxed{G=\frac{2q^2}{h}M\mathcal T}.
]

For one perfectly transmitting spin-degenerate mode,

[
\boxed{G_0=\frac{2q^2}{h}\approx 77.5\ \mu\mathrm{S}}
]

and

[
\boxed{R_0=\frac{h}{2q^2}\approx 12.9\ \mathrm{k\Omega}}.
]

This is the conductance quantum.

2.1 From one dimension to two and three dimensions

Higher-dimensional transport can be viewed as many parallel one-dimensional longitudinal modes.

2.1.1 One dimension

A strictly 1D conductor has no transverse momentum. The Fermi surface consists of two points, (\pm k_F). With total degeneracy (g),

[
\boxed{n_{1D}=\frac{gk_F}{\pi}}.
]

2.1.2 Two dimensions

For an isotropic 2D parabolic band,

[
E=\frac{\hbar^2}{2m^*}(k_x^2+k_y^2).
]

At (T=0), occupied states form a Fermi disk,

[
k_x^2+k_y^2\le k_F^2.
]

For a channel of width (W), periodic boundary conditions give

[
\Delta k_y=\frac{2\pi}{W}.
]

The allowed transverse interval is approximately (-k_F<k_y<k_F), therefore

[
M\approx\frac{2k_F}{2\pi/W}=\boxed{\frac{Wk_F}{\pi}}.
]

Including degeneracy,

[
\boxed{M_{2D}=g\frac{Wk_F}{\pi}}.
]

Each (k)-state occupies an area ((2\pi)^2/A) in 2D momentum space. Thus

[
N=g\frac{A}{(2\pi)^2}\pi k_F^2,
]

and

[
\boxed{n_{2D}=\frac{gk_F^2}{4\pi}}.
]

Therefore

[
k_F=\sqrt{\frac{4\pi n_{2D}}{g}},
\qquad
M_{2D}\propto W\sqrt{n_{2D}}.
]

Hence the width-normalized quantum contact resistance scales as

[
\boxed{R_QW\propto n_{2D}^{-1/2}}.
]

2.1.3 Three dimensions

For a 3D parabolic band,

[
E=\frac{\hbar^2}{2m^*}(k_x^2+k_y^2+k_z^2).
]

The occupied states form a Fermi sphere. Each (k)-state occupies ((2\pi)^3/V), so

[
N=g\frac{V}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{4\pi k_F^3}{3}
]

and

[
\boxed{n_{3D}=\frac{gk_F^3}{6\pi^2}}.
]

For transport along (x), each allowed pair ((k_y,k_z)) defines one longitudinal mode. For cross-sectional area (A),

[
\boxed{M_{3D}=g\frac{Ak_F^2}{4\pi}}.
]

Because (k_F\propto n_{3D}^{1/3}),

[
\boxed{M_{3D}\propto A n_{3D}^{2/3}},
\qquad
\boxed{R_QA\propto n_{3D}^{-2/3}}.
]

A compact summary is:

Dimension Fermi surface Carrier density Transport modes
1D two points (n_{1D}\propto k_F) one per occupied subband
2D circle (n_{2D}\propto k_F^2) (M\propto Wk_F)
3D sphere (n_{3D}\propto k_F^3) (M\propto Ak_F^2)

3. Transmission and Schottky barriers

In the ideal ballistic limit,

[
\mathcal T(E)=1.
]

In a real FET,

[
0\le \mathcal T(E)\le 1.
]

Transmission can be reduced by metal/semiconductor barriers, wavefunction mismatch, interface disorder, phonons, impurities, localization, and grain boundaries.

A Schottky barrier is one especially important mechanism that makes (\mathcal T(E)) strongly energy dependent.

3.1 From Landauer transmission to a Schottky barrier

For an ideal metal/n-type-semiconductor contact, the Schottky-Mott barrier height is

[
\boxed{\Phi_{Bn}=\Phi_M-\chi},
]

where (\Phi_M) is the metal work function and (\chi) is the semiconductor electron affinity.

Real interfaces may exhibit Fermi-level pinning, interface states, and metal-induced gap states, so the actual barrier can differ substantially from this ideal value.

The essential transport consequence is:

  • for (E>q\Phi_B), electrons can classically go over the barrier;
  • for (E<q\Phi_B), classical transmission is forbidden but quantum tunneling remains possible.

This leads naturally to thermionic emission (TE), thermionic-field emission (TFE), and field emission (FE).

4. Thermionic emission, field emission, and TFE

For a 3D non-degenerate semiconductor, the forward carrier flux over the barrier is obtained by integrating the occupied states with positive velocity normal to the interface:

[
J=q\int_{v_x>v_{\min}} v_x f(E)D(\mathbf k)d^3k.
]

Using the Maxwell-Boltzmann approximation

[
f(E)\approx\exp\left[-\frac{E-E_F}{k_BT}\right]
]

and the condition that the normal kinetic energy exceeds the barrier,

[
\frac{\hbar^2k_x^2}{2m^*}>q\Phi_B,
]

one obtains the Richardson equation

[
\boxed{J_S=A^*T^2\exp\left(-\frac{q\Phi_B}{k_BT}\right)}
]

with

[
\boxed{A^*=\frac{4\pi qm^*k_B^2}{h^3}}.
]

Under applied bias,

[
\boxed{J=A^*T^2\exp\left(-\frac{q\Phi_B}{k_BT}\right)\left[\exp\left(\frac{qV}{nk_BT}\right)-1\right]}.
]

The key factor

[
\exp\left(-\frac{q\Phi_B}{k_BT}\right)
]

is the fraction of the thermal distribution capable of reaching the top of the barrier.

For an ideal parabolic system of dimensionality (d), the thermionic prefactor scales approximately as

[
J_d\propto T^{(d+1)/2}e^{-q\Phi_B/k_BT}.
]

Thus, ideally,

[
1D:\ I\propto T e^{-q\Phi_B/k_BT},
]

[
2D:\ I/W\propto T^{3/2}e^{-q\Phi_B/k_BT},
]

[
3D:\ J\propto T^2e^{-q\Phi_B/k_BT}.
]

For atomically thin materials, the exact prefactor depends on contact geometry, momentum conservation, and band structure, so the conventional 3D Richardson form should not be used blindly.

4.1 Tunneling through the Schottky barrier

For a classically forbidden region (U(x)>E), the WKB transmission probability is

[
\boxed{\mathcal T(E)\approx\exp\left[-2\int_{x_1}^{x_2}\kappa(x)dx\right]}
]

with

[
\boxed{\kappa(x)=\frac{\sqrt{2m^*[U(x)-E]}}{\hbar}}.
]

For a rectangular barrier of width (w),

[
\boxed{\mathcal T(E)\approx\exp\left[-\frac{2w}{\hbar}\sqrt{2m^*(U_0-E)}\right]}.
]

Thus, barrier width is as important as barrier height.

For a triangular barrier created by a strong electric field,

[
U(x)=U_0-qFx,
]

WKB gives

[
\boxed{\mathcal T_{FE}\approx\exp\left[-\frac{4\sqrt{2m^*}(U_0-E)^{3/2}}{3q\hbar F}\right]}.
]

Hence larger electric field or stronger contact doping can dramatically increase transmission.

4.2 TE, TFE, and FE as one Landauer problem

The same Landauer integral describes all three regimes:

[
\boxed{I=\frac{2q}{h}\int M(E)\mathcal T(E)[f_S-f_D]dE}.
]

The difference is the dominant energy window.

4.2.1 Thermionic emission (TE)

Electrons mainly occupy states above the barrier:

[
E\gtrsim U_B.
]

4.2.2 Field emission (FE)

Electrons remain close to the Fermi energy,

[
E\approx E_F<U_B,
]

and tunnel directly through a thin barrier.

4.2.3 Thermionic-field emission (TFE)

Electrons first gain thermal energy but do not reach the barrier top, then tunnel through the remaining barrier:

[
E_F<E<U_B.
]

Thus TE, TFE, and FE are not three unrelated mechanisms; they are different parts of the same energy-resolved transmission integral.

5. Carrier density and contact resistance

Increasing carrier density near a FET contact has two distinct effects.

First,

[
n\uparrow\Rightarrow k_F\uparrow\Rightarrow M\uparrow,
]

so the quantum contact resistance decreases.

Second,

[
n\uparrow\Rightarrow w_B\downarrow\Rightarrow \mathcal T\uparrow,
]

so the actual contact approaches the quantum limit more closely.

Therefore

[
\boxed{n\uparrow\Rightarrow{M\uparrow,\ \mathcal T\uparrow}\Rightarrow R_c\downarrow}.
]

6. Localized states and hopping transport

The Schottky picture assumes that once the carrier enters the semiconductor, it can occupy extended states. Disorder, defects, structural randomness, strong electron-phonon coupling, or band-tail states may instead produce localized states.

A localized wavefunction decays approximately as

[
|\psi(r)|\propto e^{-r/\xi},
]

where (\xi) is the localization length.

Two localized states separated by distance (R) have overlap approximately

[
e^{-R/\xi},
]

so the transition probability contains

[
\boxed{P_{\rm overlap}\propto e^{-2R/\xi}}.
]

At this point the relevant transport process is no longer coherent propagation through an extended band, but hopping between localized states.

6.1 Phonon-assisted hopping

Consider localized states (i) and (j), separated by (R_{ij}), with energy difference (\Delta E=E_j-E_i>0). A simplified hopping rate is

[
\boxed{\Gamma_{ij}\sim\Gamma_0\exp\left(-\frac{2R_{ij}}{\xi}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{\Delta E}{k_BT}\right)}.
]

The first exponential is the spatial-overlap penalty; the second is the thermal activation penalty.

The electron therefore trades spatial distance against energy difference.

6.2 Nearest-neighbor hopping

At relatively high temperature, the electron mainly hops to nearby sites. If the characteristic activation energy is (E_A),

[
\boxed{\sigma=\sigma_0\exp\left(-\frac{E_A}{k_BT}\right)}.
]

Therefore

[
\ln\sigma\propto -\frac{1}{T}.
]

At lower temperature, it can be favorable to hop farther in real space to find a state closer in energy. This produces variable-range hopping.

6.3 Mott variable-range hopping

Assume that localized states near the Fermi energy have an approximately constant density of states (g(E_F)).

If the electron searches within radius (R) and energy interval (\Delta E), the number of candidate states in (d) dimensions scales as

[
N\sim g(E_F)\Delta E R^d.
]

For approximately one available state,

[
N\sim 1,
]

so

[
\boxed{\Delta E\sim\frac{1}{g(E_F)R^d}}.
]

The hopping exponent is

[
S(R)=\frac{2R}{\xi}+\frac{\Delta E}{k_BT}
]

or

[
S(R)=\frac{2R}{\xi}+\frac{1}{g(E_F)k_BTR^d}.
]

The optimum hop minimizes (S(R)):

[
\frac{dS}{dR}=0.
]

Therefore

[
R_{\rm opt}^{d+1}\propto\frac{\xi}{g(E_F)k_BT},
]

so

[
R_{\rm opt}\propto T^{-1/(d+1)}.
]

Substitution back into the exponent gives Mott’s law:

[
\boxed{\sigma(T)=\sigma_0\exp\left[-\left(\frac{T_0}{T}\right)^{1/(d+1)}\right]}.
]

Here

[
\boxed{T_0\sim\frac{\beta_d}{k_Bg(E_F)\xi^d}}.
]

6.4 Hopping in one, two, and three dimensions

For 1D Mott VRH,

[
\boxed{\sigma=\sigma_0\exp\left[-\left(\frac{T_0}{T}\right)^{1/2}\right]}.
]

For 2D Mott VRH,

[
\boxed{\sigma=\sigma_0\exp\left[-\left(\frac{T_0}{T}\right)^{1/3}\right]}.
]

For 3D Mott VRH,

[
\boxed{\sigma=\sigma_0\exp\left[-\left(\frac{T_0}{T}\right)^{1/4}\right]}.
]

Dimension Mott exponent
1D (1/2)
2D (1/3)
3D (1/4)

The exponent decreases with dimension because a higher-dimensional system offers more spatial possibilities for finding an energetically favorable destination.

A (T^{-1/2}) law does not automatically prove 1D hopping. Electron-electron interactions can create a Coulomb gap and produce Efros-Shklovskii VRH,

[
\boxed{\sigma=\sigma_0\exp\left[-\left(\frac{T_{ES}}{T}\right)^{1/2}\right]},
]

in multiple dimensions.

7. Mobility edges and transport in real FETs

Disorder can create localized tail states near a band edge. A useful picture is

[
\text{deep gap}\rightarrow\text{localized tail states}\rightarrow E_M\rightarrow\text{extended states},
]

where (E_M) is the mobility edge.

If the Fermi level lies below (E_M), carriers may thermally activate to extended states,

[
\boxed{\sigma\propto\exp\left[-\frac{E_M-E_F}{k_BT}\right]},
]

or hop through localized states,

[
\boxed{\sigma\propto\exp\left[-\left(\frac{T_0}{T}\right)^p\right]}.
]

For Mott VRH,

[
p=\frac{1}{d+1}.
]

A gate voltage can therefore move a FET through qualitatively different transport regimes by shifting (E_F) relative to localized and extended states.

7.1 The full transport picture of a real FET

A two-terminal FET generally contains at least three regions:

[
\boxed{R_{\rm total}=R_{c,S}+R_{\rm channel}+R_{c,D}}.
]

The source contact may be dominated by TE, TFE, or FE. The channel may be ballistic, diffusive, thermally activated, or hopping dominated. The drain introduces a second injection/extraction process.

Therefore a measured (I_D-V_G) curve may contain multiple mechanisms simultaneously:

  • Schottky injection,
  • tunneling,
  • trap filling,
  • band-tail transport,
  • hopping,
  • ordinary drift-diffusion.

This is especially important in atomically thin FETs.

8. Experimental signatures and unified picture

Mechanism Typical temperature dependence
3D thermionic emission (J/T^2\propto e^{-q\Phi_B/k_BT})
Ideal 2D thermionic emission (J/T^{3/2}\propto e^{-q\Phi_B/k_BT})
Field emission weak temperature dependence, strong field dependence
Nearest-neighbor hopping (\ln\sigma\propto-T^{-1})
1D Mott VRH (\ln\sigma\propto-T^{-1/2})
2D Mott VRH (\ln\sigma\propto-T^{-1/3})
3D Mott VRH (\ln\sigma\propto-T^{-1/4})
Efros-Shklovskii VRH (\ln\sigma\propto-T^{-1/2})

One linearized fit is rarely sufficient to establish a transport mechanism.

Useful complementary experiments include channel-length scaling, four-probe measurements, TLM, gate-dependent activation-energy analysis, bias-dependent temperature measurements, contact-metal comparison, Hall measurements, STS, and KPFM.

8.1 A unified view: from (\mathcal T=1) to hopping

The complete hierarchy is

[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
&\text{quantization of }k\
&\downarrow\
&d\dot N=\frac{dE}{h}\
&\downarrow\
&G_0=\frac{2q^2}{h}\
&\downarrow\
&G=\frac{2q^2}{h}\sum_n\mathcal T_n\
&\downarrow\
&\text{energy-dependent }\mathcal T(E)\
&\downarrow\
&\text{Schottky barrier}\
&\downarrow\
&\text{TE/TFE/FE}\
&\downarrow\
&\text{localized band-tail states}\
&\downarrow\
&\Gamma_{ij}\sim e^{-2R/\xi-\Delta E/k_BT}\
&\downarrow\
&\text{NNH/Mott VRH/ES-VRH}.
\end{aligned}}
]

The two central quantities are:

  1. Phase space: how many electronic states are available? This determines the number of Landauer modes and the quantum conductance limit.
  2. Transition probability: how easily can an electron move between those states? For extended states this is described by (\mathcal T(E)); for localized states it is described by transition rates (\Gamma_{ij}).

Seen this way, ballistic transport, Schottky injection, tunneling, and hopping are not isolated textbook models.

They are successive regimes that emerge as the nature of the electronic states and the probability of moving between them change.
